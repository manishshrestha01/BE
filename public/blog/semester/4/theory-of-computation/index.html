Introduction

Theory of Computation in Semester 4 provides the formal mathematical foundation of computer science. In Pokhara University BE Computer Engineering, this course explains how languages are defined, how machines recognize them, and what limits exist in algorithmic solvability. It helps students move from coding-level intuition to proof-level reasoning about what computers can and cannot compute.

The syllabus starts with language basics and proof techniques, then builds through finite automata and regular languages into context-free grammars and pushdown automata. These units are essential for understanding parsing, compiler design foundations, and formal system modeling. Students also learn equivalence and closure properties, which are key for exam proofs and conceptual clarity.

The final units introduce Turing machines, undecidability, and complexity classes such as P, NP, and NP-complete problems. This part of the course defines computational boundaries and gives a rigorous way to discuss hardness of problems. With regular practice in formal definitions, constructions, and proofs, TOC becomes a high-value theoretical subject rather than a memorization burden.

What you will learn

  • Use set, relation, and proof techniques in formal language context.
  • Model and analyze regular languages using DFA, NFA, and regular expressions.
  • Apply pumping lemma, closure, and decision properties for regular languages.
  • Design and simplify CFGs and convert to CNF/GNF forms.
  • Relate PDA and CFG through equivalence concepts.
  • Understand Turing machine computation and recursively enumerable languages.
  • Explain undecidability with Church-Turing thesis and halting problem.
  • Differentiate complexity classes and interpret NP-complete problem meaning.

Syllabus Overview

Unit 1: Introduction (4 hrs.)

  • Review of set, relation, and function.
  • Proof techniques: contradiction, pigeonhole principle, induction, diagonalization.
  • Alphabets and languages.
  • Chomsky hierarchy.

Unit 2: Finite Automata and Regular Language (10 hrs)

  • Deterministic and non-deterministic finite automata.
  • Regular expressions and equivalence of regular language and finite automata.
  • Properties of regular languages.
  • Pumping lemma for regular sets.
  • Closure properties and decision algorithms for regular sets.

Unit 3: Context-Free Language and Pushdown Automata (13 hrs)

  • Context-free grammar fundamentals.
  • Derivation trees and CFG simplification.
  • Normal forms: CNF and GNF.
  • Pushdown automata formal description and final-state PDA design.
  • Equivalence of PDA and CFG.
  • CFL properties, pumping lemma, closure properties, and decision algorithms.

Unit 4: Turing Machines (10 hrs)

  • Introduction to Turing machines and computation model.
  • Computing with Turing machines.
  • Extensions of Turing machines.
  • Unrestricted grammar.
  • Recursively enumerable languages.

Unit 5: Undecidability (4 hrs)

  • Church-Turing thesis.
  • Halting problem.
  • Universal Turing machines.
  • Undecidable problems about Turing machines.
  • Properties of recursive and recursively enumerable languages.

Unit 6: Computational Complexity (4 hrs)

  • Introduction to complexity theory, tractable and intractable problems.
  • Class P and class NP problems.
  • NP-complete problems.

Important Topics

  • Formal proof techniques in language and automata problems.
  • DFA/NFA design and conversion logic.
  • Regular expression and automata equivalence.
  • Pumping lemma usage for non-regular proofs.
  • CFG simplification and CNF/GNF transformation.
  • PDA construction and CFG-PDA equivalence.
  • Turing machine design and language recognition.
  • Halting problem and undecidability argument style.
  • Recursive vs recursively enumerable distinction.
  • P, NP, and NP-complete conceptual boundaries.

Practice Questions

  1. Design DFA and NFA for a given regular language and convert NFA to DFA.
  2. Construct regular expression for a language and verify equivalence with automaton.
  3. Use pumping lemma to prove that a language is not regular.
  4. Design CFG for a language and convert it to CNF or GNF.
  5. Construct PDA for a CFL and explain acceptance logic.
  6. Design a Turing machine for a basic language-recognition problem.
  7. Explain undecidability of halting problem using formal argument outline.
  8. Classify example problems into P, NP, and NP-complete categories with reasoning.
