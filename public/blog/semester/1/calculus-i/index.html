Introduction

Calculus I in Semester 1 of Pokhara University BE Computer Engineering is the bridge between school-level mathematics and engineering-level analytical thinking. The course is not only about learning formulas; it trains you to describe change, behavior, and constraints in a precise way. When you study limits, continuity, and derivatives, you are building the language used to model sensitivity, growth, and variation in engineering systems. This is why the first unit emphasizes conceptual clarity before heavy computation. If the logic of limits and differentiability is strong, many later topics become easier to understand and solve. The same foundation also supports future subjects in optimization, signals, and data-driven computation.

As the syllabus progresses, you move from core derivative rules to applied analysis through mean value theorems, Taylor and Maclaurin expansion, asymptotes, curve tracing, and curvature. These topics are central because they teach approximation, interpretation, and graphical reasoning. The integral calculus units then shift focus from rate of change to accumulation. Definite and improper integrals, reduction formulas, and Beta-Gamma functions are included to prepare you for both theoretical questions and application-based exam problems. You will also use integration for geometric quantities like area, arc length, surface area, and volume of solids of revolution, which makes the mathematics directly relevant to engineering contexts.

The final part of the course introduces multivariable methods and ordinary differential equations. Partial differentiation, extrema, and Lagrange multipliers develop constrained optimization skills, while first-order and second-order differential equations introduce dynamic system modeling. These units are highly valuable in later engineering analysis because they connect mathematical tools with real processes. If you follow a consistent strategy of concept review, tutorial practice, and stepwise derivation writing, Calculus I becomes a scoring subject rather than a difficult one. This guide is organized unit-wise to help you build that consistency and prepare effectively for internal assessments and semester-end examination.

What you will learn

  • Use limits, continuity, and differentiability to analyze function behavior correctly.
  • Compute higher-order derivatives using Leibnitz method for product functions.
  • Apply Rolle's theorem and Lagrange's theorem to solve mean value problems.
  • Expand functions using Taylor and Maclaurin series for approximation tasks.
  • Evaluate definite and improper integrals, including reduction formula and Beta-Gamma applications.
  • Compute area, arc length, surface area, and volume using integration methods.
  • Work with partial derivatives, total derivatives, and implicit differentiation.
  • Solve first-order and second-order ODEs for engineering modeling scenarios.

Syllabus Overview

Unit I: Limit, Continuity and Derivatives (5 hrs)

  • Introduction to differential calculus in engineering.
  • Limit, continuity, and differentiability of functions.
  • Higher-order derivatives by Leibnitz method.

Unit II: Applications of Derivatives (8 hrs)

  • Mean value theorems: Rolle's theorem and Lagrange's theorem with geometric interpretation.
  • Higher-order mean value theorem, Taylor series, and Maclaurin series expansion.
  • Asymptotes to Cartesian curves up to fourth degree.
  • Curve tracing in Cartesian and parametric forms.
  • Curvature and its interpretation.

Unit III: Integral Calculus (6 hrs)

  • Introduction and review of indefinite integration.
  • Fundamental theorem of integral calculus.
  • Definite integral and its properties.
  • Improper integrals and comparison test.
  • Reduction formula and Beta-Gamma functions.

Unit IV: Application of Integral (6 hrs)

  • Area under a curve and area between two curves.
  • Arc length for Cartesian and parametric curves.
  • Surface area and volume of solids of revolution.

Unit V: Partial Differentiation (3 hrs)

  • Introduction to partial derivatives.
  • Homogeneous functions and Euler's theorem for two and three variables.
  • Total derivatives and implicit function differentiation.

Unit VI: Application of Partial Differentiation (4 hrs)

  • Extrema of functions of two and three variables.
  • Lagrange's method of undetermined multipliers (up to two multipliers).

Unit VII: First-Order Ordinary Differential Equations (6 hrs)

  • Review of separable, homogeneous, and exact differential equations with engineering applications.
  • Linear, Bernoulli, and Riccati equations with engineering applications.
  • Mathematical modeling of engineering problems using first-order equations.

Unit VIII: Second-Order Ordinary Differential Equations (7 hrs)

  • Second-order homogeneous ODE with constant and variable coefficients, including Euler-Cauchy equation.
  • Existence and uniqueness of solution, Wronskian, and general solution framework.
  • Non-homogeneous second-order ODE by undetermined coefficients and variation of parameters.
  • Engineering applications of second-order differential equation models.

Important Topics

  • Limit evaluation methods and continuity test at critical points.
  • Differentiability condition and its relation to continuity.
  • Leibnitz theorem for nth derivative of product terms.
  • Rolle's theorem and Lagrange theorem with geometric meaning.
  • Taylor and Maclaurin expansion for approximation and error reduction.
  • Asymptotes, curve tracing, and curvature for qualitative graph analysis.
  • Definite, improper, and reduction-based integral techniques.
  • Beta and Gamma functions and their conversion identities.
  • Partial derivatives, Euler's theorem, and total differentiation.
  • Lagrange multipliers, first-order ODE forms, and second-order ODE solution methods.

Practice Questions

  1. Prove that differentiability implies continuity, and give one example where continuity does not imply differentiability.
  2. Using Leibnitz theorem, find the nth derivative of (x^2 + 1)e^x and simplify the final expression.
  3. Verify Rolle's theorem and Lagrange's theorem for a suitable polynomial in a closed interval, then interpret the result geometrically.
  4. Expand ln(1 + x) and sin x using Maclaurin series up to required terms and compare approximation quality near x = 0.
  5. Evaluate one definite and one improper integral, then justify convergence of the improper integral using comparison test.
  6. Find area between two curves and compute arc length of a given parametric curve over a fixed interval.
  7. For a two-variable function, compute critical points and classify extrema; then solve one constrained extrema problem using Lagrange multipliers.
  8. Solve one first-order linear differential equation and one non-homogeneous second-order equation using an appropriate method, then interpret their engineering meaning.
